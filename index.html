EOB project · Bio-inspired structures

Nature solved this already.

Honey bees and spiders work with limited wax and protein and still carry real loads. Two simulations below recreate that trade-off between strength, weight and material, so you can over-build, under-build and find the point in between.

The engineering challenge

Build too much and you waste it. Build too little and it fails.

Modern construction and manufacturing struggle to balance strength, weight and material cost. Over-built structures waste material, add cost and increase the carbon footprint. Under-built ones fail under load, wind or impact.

34%of global CO₂ emissions come from buildings and construction

Figures as cited in the EOB project research.

Can we design structures that are:

  • Light, yet able to bear heavy or dynamic loads
  • Material and cost efficient, minimising weight
  • Resilient to impact, vibration, wind and seismic force
  • Scalable, from a packaging box to a stadium roof
  • Sustainable, using less energy and raw material to build
Part one · Honeycomb

Honeycomb crush test

Drop a weight onto the same mass of aluminium, built two ways. The solid slab is a few millimetres thick and stops the weight almost instantly. The honeycomb is deep, and its walls fold one row at a time, holding the force steady while the weight slows down.

Why the geometry works

Many small members beat a few big ones.

Traditional frames rely on a few strong beams. Both structures here split the job across many connected members, which improves resilience while lowering mass.

Honeycomb

Compression and energy absorption
  • Short, shared wallsAdjacent cells share walls and brace each other, forming a multi-path truss. Each hexagon shares the strain, so a local failure does not spread.
  • Progressive collapseCells deform row by row and absorb mechanical energy instead of failing all at once. This is what the crush test shows.
  • Low density, useful stiffnessCellular geometry needs less material for a component with useful mechanical performance, and designs can be graded, hierarchical or reinforced.

Spider web

Tension and redundancy
  • Tension carries the loadRadial threads take the main load and spiral threads spread forces across the web, much like a cable net or a suspension bridge.
  • Redundancy and damage toleranceWhen one strand breaks, the rest redistribute the load. Cut threads in the test to see it. The same idea is used in earthquake-resistant design.
  • Form follows loadThread arrangement changes strength. Engineers use form-finding and optimisation algorithms to reach efficient shapes the way spiders reach them by instinct.

Limits

Where the idea gets hard.

Real constraints

  • Manufacturing complexity. Hierarchical cell geometries and web-like lattices often need advanced manufacturing such as 3D printing.
  • Direction-dependent behaviour. Honeycomb performance changes with the direction and type of loading.
  • Local damage. Damage to individual cells changes the response of the whole structure.
  • Scaling. A design that works in the lab cannot be assumed to work the same way in a full-sized building. Much of the web research covers materials, lattices and components, not complete buildings.

What these simulations leave out

  • Honeycomb test. A one-dimensional crush model using scaling laws for in-plane collapse (plateau stress proportional to the square of wall thickness over cell size, densification near 1 − 1.4 × relative density). Aluminium values are typical, not measured. Magnitudes are illustrative.
  • Web test. A tension-only mass and spring model with silk-like stretch and a break strain of 28%. Weight is in relative units. Anchors are rigid and air drag is ignored.
  • Shape comparison. Counts wall length for equal-area cells in the thin-wall limit. Load-path colours are a schematic, not a simulation.
  • Both. Good for seeing how the trade-off behaves. Not a substitute for finite element analysis or testing.
